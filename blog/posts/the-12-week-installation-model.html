01 / The Expanding Engagement

Why consulting engagements never end

Traditional consulting has a structural problem. The incentive model rewards duration, not delivery.

A six-month discovery phase produces a deck. The deck recommends a three-month implementation. Implementation reveals complexity that was not in the original scope. Scope expands. Timeline extends. A new workstream opens. Twelve months in, the consultants are still on-site. The retainer renews. The dependency deepens.

This is not an accident. It is the business model.

Retainer-based consulting creates three compounding problems:

  • Incentive misalignment. The consulting firm profits from staying longer. The client profits from the consultants leaving. These incentives are structurally opposed.
  • Scope creep as a feature. Every discovery reveals adjacent work. Without a fixed boundary, every adjacent problem becomes billable. The engagement grows because no one draws the line.
  • Dependency by design. When the consulting team holds the knowledge, the institutional memory, and the operating context, the client cannot function without them. That is not a partnership. It is a dependency.

The result: the average enterprise consulting engagement runs 14 months. Most of the value is delivered in the first 90 days. Everything after that is maintenance of the relationship, not the system.

02 / Parkinson's Law

Compression produces better outcomes

Parkinson's Law is not a theory. It is a diagnosis. Work expands to fill the time available for its completion.

Give a team six months to design an operating model and they will use six months. Give them three weeks and they will design the same model — with fewer meetings, fewer revision cycles, and fewer committees involved in decisions that should take one person ten minutes.

The constraint is not the enemy of good work. It is the condition that produces it.

Compression forces four behaviours that open-ended timelines prevent:

  • Faster decisions. When there is no next quarter to defer to, decisions happen in the room. The approval cycle that takes three weeks in a six-month project takes three days in a twelve-week installation.
  • Tighter feedback loops. A pilot that runs in weeks 10-12 produces real data before the engagement ends. That data validates the design or forces a correction — while the team is still in the building, not six months later.
  • Reduced coordination overhead. Fewer people need to be involved when fewer meetings need to be scheduled. Compression eliminates the status updates, steering committees, and alignment sessions that exist only because the timeline allows them.
  • Scope discipline. When the boundary is fixed, every decision about what to include is also a decision about what to exclude. That is not a limitation. That is design.
03 / The Three Phases

Design. Build. Pilot.

Every Optiflows installation follows the same three-phase architecture. The phases are not arbitrary. Each one produces a specific output that feeds the next.

PhaseWeeksOutput
Design1–3Operating model blueprint — workflows mapped, decision points defined, system architecture documented
Build4–9Deployed infrastructure — automations wired, dashboards built, campaign engine configured
Pilot10–12Validated system — live data flowing, team running the model, adjustments made from real usage

Design (Weeks 1–3)

We map how work currently moves. Not how it is described in process documents — how it actually moves. Who routes decisions. Where approvals stall. Which handoffs create delays. The output is a system blueprint: the operating model that will replace the current one.

Three weeks is enough to diagnose and design. Most of what a six-month discovery phase produces is consensus-building, not insight. The insight is usually visible in the first two weeks. The remaining four months are spent socialising it.

Build (Weeks 4–9)

We wire the infrastructure. Automations that move pipeline through logic instead of manual handoffs. Dashboards that show what to do, not just what happened. Campaign architecture that produces repeatable output instead of one-off launches.

Six weeks of build time forces prioritisation. We install the system that handles 80% of the coordination load. The remaining 20% is edge cases that the team handles with the new model as their operating layer.

Pilot (Weeks 10–12)

The system runs with live data and the actual team. Not a test environment. Not a simulation. The real operating model, running in production, while we are still present to adjust.

This is the phase that most traditional engagements skip entirely. They hand over a deck and leave. We hand over a running system and validate it before we go.

04 / Fixed Scope, Defined Deliverables

What "fixed scope" actually means

Fixed scope does not mean fixed activities. It means fixed deliverables.

The distinction matters. A fixed-activity contract says: "We will run 12 workshops, produce 4 strategy documents, and hold 6 steering committee meetings." The activities are defined. The outcomes are not.

A fixed-deliverable contract says: "At the end of 12 weeks, you will have a deployed campaign engine, an automated pipeline flow, and a live insights dashboard." The outputs are defined. How we get there adapts to what we find.

The anti-retainer model

We do not sell ongoing access to our team. We install infrastructure that runs without us. The system stays when we leave. If a system requires the people who built it to remain permanently, it is not a system. It is a service. Infrastructure compounds. Advice expires.

This is why the scope is fixed to deliverables, not activities. The activities flex based on what the team needs during the installation. The deliverables do not flex because they are the architecture. You are not paying for our time. You are paying for a system that works after our time is over.
