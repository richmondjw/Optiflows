Flow Optimisation Is Not Process Improvement

One polishes the existing shape. The other redesigns how work moves.
Systems Thinking  ·  20 Feb 2026  ·  5 min read
Bottom line: Process improvement assumes the current process is the right shape. It asks "how do we make this faster?" Flow optimisation starts from first principles: "how should work actually move through this system?" One polishes. The other redesigns. Most scaling companies need the second and keep doing the first.
01 / The Process Improvement Trap

Making a bad system faster

A company notices that deals take too long to close. The response is predictable: map the sales process, find the slow steps, speed them up. Add automation to the proposal stage. Reduce the number of approval steps from three to two. Shave two days off the average cycle.

This is process improvement. It works on the assumption that the process is the right shape and just needs to be faster. But what if the process itself is the problem? What if proposals should not require approval at all? What if the handoff between sales and delivery creates three days of latency that no amount of step-level acceleration will fix?

Process improvement polishes the surface. It makes a broken system run more efficiently. But an efficient broken system is still broken. It just breaks faster.

We redesign how growth work happens.
02 / Why Lean and Six Sigma Miss the Frame

The wrong frame at the wrong stage

Lean, Six Sigma, and continuous improvement methodologies were built for manufacturing. They work when the production architecture is fixed and the goal is to reduce variance within it. An assembly line has a defined shape. The work is to make each station faster and more consistent.

Scaling companies do not have a fixed production architecture. The operating model is still forming. Work moves through informal channels, tribal knowledge, and founder-mediated decisions. Applying Lean to this environment is like measuring defect rates on a factory floor that has not been built yet.

The problem is not that these methodologies are bad. The problem is that they assume the frame is correct and work to improve within it. At a company between 10 and 50 people, the frame itself is usually wrong. The way work routes between functions, the way decisions get made, the way information flows from one team to the next — all of this needs redesigning, not refining.

Continuous improvement compounds when the architecture is right. It stalls when the architecture is wrong. Most scaling companies are improving inside the wrong architecture.

03 / Flow vs. Process

The physics of work movement

A process is the documented sequence of steps to complete a task. Write proposal. Send for approval. Revise. Send to client. Follow up. It describes what happens.

A flow is the physics of how work moves through a system. Where does it queue? Where does it wait? Where does it hand off between people? Where does information get lost in the transfer? Flow describes how work actually behaves — not how it is supposed to behave.

Process lives in documentation. Flow lives in reality. The gap between the two is where most operational drag hides.

Three structural causes of slow work

Dependencies: Work cannot start until something else finishes, even when there is no logical reason for the sequence. Queues: Work sits waiting for a person, a review, or a decision — the wait time often exceeds the work time. Handoffs: Work moves between people or teams, losing context at every transfer. Each handoff is a chance for delay, error, and information loss.

Process improvement looks at each step and asks: how do we make this step faster? Flow analysis looks at the connections between steps and asks: should this dependency exist? Should this queue exist? Should this handoff exist at all?

04 / First Principles Before Improvement

Ask whether the work should move this way at all

Before improving anything, ask a different question: should this work move through the system in this shape?

A client onboarding process with 14 steps and 4 handoffs is not waiting to be made faster. It is waiting to be redesigned. The first-principles question is not "how do we speed up step 7?" It is "why does this work touch 4 different people, and can it touch 2?"

Flow optimisation starts here. Not with a stopwatch on existing steps, but with a structural question about how work should route through the system. The answer often looks nothing like the current process.

In practice, this means:

  • Removing routing steps — if a person's only role is to receive work and pass it to the next person, that step is latency, not value
  • Automating decision points — if a decision follows a consistent pattern (thresholds, criteria, rules), wire it into the system so it executes without waiting for a human
  • Eliminating approval chains — most approvals exist because trust has not been encoded into the system. Define the boundaries. Let the team act within them.
05 / The Operating Model as the Unit of Redesign

Not the process — the connections

Process improvement works at the task level. Flow optimisation works at the system level. The unit of redesign is not the individual process. It is the operating model — the entire architecture of how work moves through the company.

The output of marketing is the input of sales. The output of sales is the input of delivery. The output of delivery is the input of customer success. Each function produces something the next function consumes. The quality, speed, and structure of that handoff determines how fast the whole system moves.

You can make marketing 30% faster. But if the handoff to sales loses half the context, the system does not get 30% faster. It gets marginal improvement at one node while the constraint sits at the connection.

Marketing  ──→  Sales  ──→  Delivery  ──→  Success
         ↑          ↑            ↑
       nodes      nodes        nodes

    ─────── connections ───────
    (this is where drag lives)
The constraint is usually the connection, not the node

Systems thinking demands this perspective. You do not build a faster company by making individual functions faster. You build a faster company by redesigning how work flows between them. The connections are the architecture. The individual processes are just the components.

This is why process improvement fails at scaling companies. It works on the components while the architecture stays broken. Flow optimisation works on the architecture. The components improve as a consequence.

Infrastructure compounds. Advice expires. The companies that scale past the coordination ceiling are the ones that stop polishing individual processes and start redesigning how work moves through the entire system.
